# Fixing mistakes

The quick-lookup version of what Module 5 teaches. Every row is **symptom → cause → fix**, so you
can diagnose a fabric and act without reading the whole lesson.

The single most useful thing in this table is the first column: beginners rarely get hurt by making
a mistake, only by not recognising one. Learn to *see* the three or four most common ones.

## Fast diagnosis

| What you see | What it is | Quickest fix |
|---|---|---|
| A hole with a ladder of loose bars above it | A dropped stitch, several rows down | [Ladder down](#dropped-stitch-and-laddering) and pick it up |
| One stitch that is a `V` with a diagonal across it | A twisted stitch | Untangle it: knit the stitch, slip it, or work the back loop |
| A row that is noticeably wider or narrower than the rest | An increase or decrease you did not mean to make | Compare stitch counts at the row ends; tink back to the wrong point |
| A gap that widens from the needle outwards | The last row or round is not tight against the needle | Work the next stitch more firmly, or use a smaller needle |
| A fabric that rolls into a tube at the edges | Stockinette, which is unbalanced | Not a mistake — change the stitch pattern (see 03.2) |
| Edges that flare out in a scallop | Too few stitches at the edge, or the edge stitch is loose every row | Use a slipped selvedge, or pick up an extra edge stitch |
| A tight, puckered row between two loose rows | You changed tension for a row and did not change back | Block it, or repeat the relaxed row on the next pass |
| A hat or sleeve with a diagonal ridge running up it | The beginning of the round is not aligned with the edge | Move the beginning of the round a stitch or two into the middle of the back |

## The fixes, in detail

### Undoing one stitch — tink

For a mistake made in the last few stitches of a row, undoing single stitches is much cheaper than
frogging.

1. Insert the left needle into the stitch **in front of the right needle**, as if to knit.
2. Slide that stitch off the right needle and onto the left needle, **without working a new one**.
3. Repeat until the mistake is gone. Then re-knit the stitches you undid, working normally.

Doing it a second time is much easier than it reads, and it is the same motion as knitting in
reverse: everything un-knits, nothing frogged.

### Dropped stitch and laddering

A dropped stitch has come off the needle, and the row below shows a **ladder** — a run of loose
horizontal bars. Each bar is one row's worth of that stitch, and the dropped stitch is the one bar
at the top that still has a loop on the end.

Work **up** the ladder from the bottom, from the oldest bar to the newest, and put the dropped
stitch back on the needle each time.

1. Find the dropped loop — the topmost bar with a loop hanging on it.
2. Use a crochet hook (easiest) or the tip of your right needle to reach **through** the loop.
3. Catch the bar just below it and pull that bar up through the loop, so the loop now has one more
   bar in it.
4. Repeat for every bar down to the row where the stitch should be.
5. Put the loop back on the left needle the right way round — the same way it was before. Knitting
   the next stitch reveals whether it was a knit or a purl: a `V` means it was a knit.

> [!TIP]
> If you have no crochet hook, the right needle tip does the same job, but you must use a **very
> fine** needle for the final pickup so it passes through the loop without splitting the yarn.

### Frogging to a lifeline

When the mistake is far back, undoing a whole row is the fastest route. Do it deliberately.

1. **Put in a lifeline first.** Thread a contrasting yarn or a fine needle through the live stitches
   above the mistake. This is the one habit that turns a catastrophe into a small annoyance — run a
   lifeline every few inches and you can always come back to a known-good point.
2. Unravel back to the lifeline, stopping the moment the live stitches are back on the needle.
3. Pick the stitches back onto the needle, one at a time, **through the correct part of the loop**:
   front and back legs if the stitch faces you as a `V`, back and front legs if it faces you as a
   bump. A stitch put on the needle the wrong way looks like a `V` facing the wrong direction — that
   is the classic "my frogging looks wrong" moment.
4. Read the fabric to work out what the stitch pattern should be, and re-knit the row.

### Count your way out

Most dropped stitches happen at the ends of rows, where you are distracted by turning the work. So
the cheapest prevention is a count:

- Count your stitches at the end of every row, out loud or with fingers.
- Use a stitch marker to mark the first stitch of a row or round, and count to it every time.

If the count is wrong, find out *where* the wrongness is before you go on. A row that ends one
short has either an unnoticed decrease or a dropped stitch; a row that ends one long has an
increase or a yarn-over you did not mean.

## What is not a mistake

| It looks like | It is | Do this |
|---|---|---|
| A rolled-up stockinette rectangle | Unbalanced fabric | Leave it for a scarf, or add a border; see lesson 03.2 |
| Ribbing that looks tight near the cast-on | Ribbing needs a few rows to settle | Keep going; it opens out |
| A hat that feels loose at the brim | Negative ease working as intended | Wash and block it; wool tightens |
| A slight ridge at the start of a round | The jog | Move the round start; see lesson 08.4 |
| A slightly wavy selvedge | The edge stitch was worked loosely every row | Use a slipped edge next time |

## Prevent it, do not fix it

- **Count every row.** The single most effective habit in the craft.
- **Use a row counter** — a phone app, a clicker, or a pencil. If you stop mid-row, it tells you
  where you were.
- **Run a lifeline every few inches** of a long piece.
- **Read the fabric every few rows.** A dropped stitch is obvious immediately and invisible a week
  later.
- **Take breaks.** Most mistakes are made in the last five minutes of a session when the hands get
  tired and the attention drifts.
