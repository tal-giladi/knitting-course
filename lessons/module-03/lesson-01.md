# 03.1 · The purl stitch

The purl is the second of the two stitches everything else is built from, and it is a smaller thing
than it looks: you already have the motion, you just do it from the other side. In this lesson you
will knit a row and a purl row, and see for the first time that a choice of stitch changes the
*behaviour* of the fabric — not just its look.

## What you need

- Your yarn and 5 mm (US 8) needle.
- Your Module 2 swatch, still on the needle if you have it, or a new cast-on of 20 stitches.

## Why it works

A purl is the same loop as a knit, drawn through from the other direction. The loop itself does
not change: it is one loop of yarn, with a head, two legs and a foot. What changes is **which face
of the loop ends up pointing at you.**

That is why the two stitches look nothing alike and are still the same object. From one side a
loop shows its two legs pulled down inside it — a `V`. From the other side it shows its rounded
head — a bump. Knitting shows you the first face. Purling shows you the second. You are not making
a different stitch; you are turning the same one round.

There is one more difference between the two, and it is the important one, so keep it in mind for
the next lesson:

> A row of knits is **wider** than a row of purls holding the same number of stitches. The knits
> spread out across the row; the purls sit closer together.

On its own that is a curiosity. In the next lesson it turns out to be the reason a whole fabric
rolls itself into a tube.

## Step by step

![Three panels showing the purl stitch: the right needle enters the front of the next stitch from the right and deeper than a knit, the working yarn is brought around to the front of the right needle, and the new loop is pulled through to the front before the old stitch slides off](../../assets/m03-purl-motion.svg)

1. **Insert the right needle into the front of the next stitch, going in from the right, and a
   little further in than you would for a knit.** A purl stitch is entered more deeply than a knit
   because you need to go through to the far side of the loop. If you find yourself in exactly the
   same position as for a knit, you have probably not gone in far enough.

2. **Bring the working yarn around to the front of the right needle.** The yarn starts behind the
   work. Take it over the top of the right needle tip and round to the front, so it ends up between
   the needle and you. This is the whole difference between a purl and a knit, and it is worth
   saying out loud: *yarn to the front*.

3. **Draw the new loop through to the front**, the same way as a knit but towards you and out,
   then let the old stitch slide off the left needle tip. On the right side of the fabric, the new
   loop will look like a bump rather than a V.

To purl the next row, turn the work. The yarn is now in the correct place — front — for the first
stitch of the new row, so purls all the way down need no extra setup.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The yarn must be **in front** for the whole stitch. A purl worked with the yarn behind produces
> a loop that looks like a knit but is structurally wrong: the legs are twisted and the stitch is
> narrower than it should be. It looks fine and is not.

This is a slow, close-up demonstration of the purl stitch by itself, which is the clearest way to
see the yarn coming round to the front.

### Left-handed and Continental

Purling is where the English and Continental methods differ most, so this is the moment to try the
alternative if the standard way is not comfortable for your hands.

**Left-handed English (throwing).** Everything above is unchanged. The yarn comes from your left
hand, and you throw it over the left index finger to make slack, then round the tip of the right
needle to the front. Some left-handers find the purl easier than the knit this way, because the
yarn never crosses over the front of the work.

**Continental (picking).** The usual continental purl picks the yarn up with the needle tip from
the front, exactly as described above. Many continental knitters prefer a second option, often
called **purl as if knitting**: keep the yarn *behind* the work and scoop it through the loop from
behind, so a purl and a knit use the same hand movement. It is faster, and it is worth learning if
plain continental purling strains your wrist. In a written pattern it appears as "purl as if
knitting" or purl with the yarn in back.

**Mirror knitting.** Purl as usual; what changes is that the stitches end up on the left needle and
the row runs the other way. The fabric looks the same. The full translation table, including how
purls and the rest of the course's techniques differ for mirror knitters, is on the
[left-handed reference page](../../references/left-handed.md).

## Check your work

- **Every stitch on the right side of a purl row is a bump.** Turn the work over and the same
  stitches are `V`s. A `V` on the right side of a purl row means you knitted that stitch by
  accident.
- **A purl row looks like stacked ridges**, running horizontally. Knit rows look smooth with clear
  V's; purl rows look bumpy and slightly rope-like. The contrast between the two is the clearest
  sign that you really did change stitch.
- **Compare widths.** Lay a row of knits flat next to a row of purls of the same stitch count, and
  measure both. The purl row is narrower. Write the difference down — you will use it in the next
  lesson to explain why a fabric rolls up.
- **The first stitch of a purl row often catches.** At the turn, the yarn is in front and the tip
  can scoop the first stitch of the row below. Check it deliberately on every row for the first
  few rows, and unknit it if it happens.

## Fixing common mistakes

**The yarn stays behind and the stitch comes out too narrow.** Symptom: a purl row that looks
right but measures narrower than it should, and a stitch you can see is slightly twisted. Cause:
the yarn never came round to the front, so you pulled the loop through from the wrong side. Fix:
unknit that stitch and redo it with the yarn in front. Because the mistake is invisible from the
wrong side, "my purl row is narrower than my knit row" is the symptom to trust.

**You go in from the back, as for a knit.** Symptom: the right needle tip pokes through the back
of the stitch and splits the yarn, or the new loop comes out twisted. Cause: treating the purl like
a knit. Fix: unknit and re-enter from the right, deeper. If you are consistently struggling here,
check that you are not in fact knitting — the two feel surprisingly similar at first.

**The first purl of the row is a knit.** Symptom: a single `V` at the left edge of a row of
bumps. Cause: at the turn, the yarn was still in front from the previous row and you entered the
first stitch the way you do for a purl, so you knitted it. Fix: on the next row, knit into that
stitch as if to knit it, then purl the rest of the row normally.

## Practice

**The finish line: a swatch with five knit rows, five purl rows, and five knit rows — about 10 cm
(4") wide and 12 cm (5") tall, visibly ridged in the middle.**

1. **Cast on 20 stitches.**
2. **Knit 5 rows, purl 5 rows, knit 5 rows.** Count at the end of every row.
3. **Lay it flat and measure the knit section and the purl section separately.** Both are 5 rows
   of the same 20 stitches; they will not measure the same. Write both numbers on the swatch card.
4. **Bind off, label, and fill in the [swatch card](../../templates/swatch-card.md).** Keep it.

Then, if you have the time and the yarn, make a second swatch **knitting every row** — that is
garter stitch, and you are about to find out why it behaves so differently from this one.

> [!TIP]
> Purling takes longer than knitting and it always will. Your rhythm will be slower and your
> tension slightly firmer, which is normal and fine. Take a break every 25-30 minutes; the
> discomfort beginners feel in purling is nearly always tension, not the stitch.

## Tips from experienced knitters

- **Say "yarn to the front" every time.** It sounds silly and it is the whole trick. Most purl
  problems are a yarn that stayed behind for one stitch.
- **Purls fall out of rhythm faster than knits.** Because they are slower, you tend to speed up
  without noticing, and a row that narrows towards the end is the result. Count.
- **Do not fight the stitch to make it fast.** Speed comes from a relaxed grip and a fixed
  sequence, not from pulling harder. Knitting purls with a tight hand is how beginners get a sore
  thumb.
- **Rest the work on a table or a cushion.** Purling is a good moment to stop holding everything in
  the air, because the purl lets you lean the work forward.
- **Try purl-as-if-knitting if your wrist tires.** It is a real technique, not a compromise, and
  patterns that say "purl" can be worked either way.

## Recap

- A purl is the same loop as a knit, drawn through from the other side. It is one stitch with two
  faces, not two stitches.
- The yarn goes **to the front**. That is the entire difference, and it is worth saying out loud.
- A purl makes a bump on the right side and a `V` on the wrong side — the reverse of a knit.
- A row of purls is **narrower** than a row of knits with the same stitch count. Remember this
  number.
- Count every row, and expect purls to be slower and firmer than knits.

> The principle to keep: **a stitch's shape comes from which face of the loop you are looking at,
> and its width comes from which way you drew it through.** That second fact — that a purl row is
> narrower than a knit row — is the whole explanation for the next lesson, and the whole
> explanation for why some knitted things lie flat and some roll themselves into a tube.
